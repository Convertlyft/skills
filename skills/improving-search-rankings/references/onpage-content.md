# On-page and content — intent, titles, internal links, quality, E-E-A-T

Scope: why an indexed page underperforms for its target query, and how to fix
the page itself. Everything here assumes the technical floor holds (page is
indexed and renderable) — verify that first.

## Search intent: the verdict is on the results page

Before judging any page, search its target query and read what ranks. The
results page is Google's public statement of what people want for that query.
Classify what you see:

- **Learn** (informational): guides, explainers, answer boxes, videos.
- **Compare** (commercial research): "best X" lists, reviews, comparison
  tables, often written by third parties rather than sellers.
- **Buy** (transactional): product and category pages, shopping results, ads.
- **Go** (navigational): one brand dominates; nobody else wins these.

Decision rules:

- The page's *shape* must match the dominant shape in the results. A product
  page cannot rank for a query where every result is a buying guide, no
  matter how good the product page is. The fix is a different page, not a
  better version of the wrong one.
- Mixed results (some guides, some product pages) mean split intent — a page
  can win the minority slot, but expect a ceiling.
- Forums and community threads ranking prominently signal that searchers
  want first-hand experience and distrust polished marketing — an
  opportunity for genuinely experience-based content.
- If the results are all household-name brands, the honest advice is to
  target a longer, more specific variant of the query instead. Say this
  plainly rather than proposing a doomed content project.

Symptoms of intent mismatch in behaviour data (sessions, funnels, heatmaps, replays): decent rank data but
funnels show immediate exits; heatmaps show no scroll past the opening;
replays show people arriving, scanning briefly, and leaving. The click was
won and the promise was wrong.

## Titles and snippets

The title tag is a ranking input and the ad copy of the organic result. What
good looks like: the page's one topic stated plainly in the first words, in
the language people actually search with, unique across the site, with the
brand at the end if at all. One page, one topic, one title.

- Front-load the substance; trailing words get cut off in display.
- Google rewrites titles it considers weak. Seeing a rewritten title in the
  results is feedback: the written one was boilerplate ("Home"), stuffed
  with repeated keywords, or disconnected from the page content. Fix the
  title rather than fighting the rewrite.
- The meta description does not affect ranking. Write it as a honest pitch
  for the click, and accept that Google substitutes its own excerpt for many
  queries. Never let anyone sell meta-description rewriting as ranking work.
- The main heading on the page should agree with the title (same promise,
  not necessarily identical words). Title says X, page opens about Y — that
  mismatch costs both rankings and trust.

Snippet-worthiness: for question-shaped queries, place a direct, complete
answer in the first lines under the relevant heading — a short paragraph
that could stand alone. That is what gets pulled into answer boxes and read
aloud by assistants. Bury the answer after a long preamble and someone
else's page gets quoted.

## Internal linking

Internal links are the site's own statement of what matters and what is
about what. Google follows them for discovery and reads them for meaning.
Most sites underuse them badly.

Method:

1. **Hub and spoke.** One strong page per topic (the hub) linking to every
   specific subpage (spokes); every spoke links back to the hub and sideways
   to closely related spokes. This concentrates topical meaning and gives
   every page a path in.
2. **Descriptive anchors.** The clickable text should say what the target
   page is about — the anchor is a label Google reads. "Click here" and
   "read more" waste the signal. Vary phrasing naturally; identical
   exact-match anchors everywhere look mechanical.
3. **Link from strength to need.** Find the site's strongest pages (most
   organic sessions, most external links) and add in-content links from them
   to pages that need to rank. In-content links beat footer and sidebar
   links, which are discounted as boilerplate.
4. **Hunt orphans.** Any page meant to rank with no internal links pointing
   at it is invisible. Sitemap presence does not substitute.
5. **Prune noise.** Enormous mega-menus and footers linking everything to
   everything dilute the map. If every page links to every page, the links
   say nothing.

What bad looks like: money pages reachable only through the nav menu, blog
posts that never link to the product they discuss, and pagination as the only
path to older content.

## Content quality — the people-first test

Google's stated target is content made for people that demonstrates
first-hand knowledge; its systems reward it sitewide, meaning a mass of
low-quality pages drags down the good ones. Tests to apply to any page:

- Would this page exist, in this form, if search engines did not? Content
  reverse-engineered from a keyword list reads that way.
- Does it say anything the top results do not already say? A rewrite of the
  current winners gives Google no reason to prefer it. The winning angle is
  what only this owner can add: their data, their cases, their photographs,
  their opinion earned from doing the work.
- Does the opening deliver, or throat-clear? Heatmaps showing readers gone
  before the substance starts are the diagnostic.
- Is it sized to the need? Padding a simple answer into a long essay hurts;
  so does a thin stub on a topic that demands depth. Match the depth the
  results page shows people want.

Patterns that classify as low quality regardless of writing polish: thin
pages generated per city or per keyword variant with swapped nouns (doorway
pages), near-duplicate pages competing for one query, tag and archive pages
indexed en masse, and undisclosed mass-produced content on topics the site
has no standing in. The remedy is consolidation and pruning: merge
near-duplicates into one strong page (redirecting the losers), `noindex`
the archive noise, delete what nothing would miss.

Cannibalization check: when two of the site's pages swap in and out of rank
data for one query, they are splitting one intent. Consolidate into one page;
redirect the other.

## E-E-A-T — experience, expertise, authoritativeness, trust

E-E-A-T is the lens from Google's quality-rater guidelines, not a score or a
checklist item. It matters most on YMYL topics — money, health, safety,
law — where Google is visibly harsher on unaccredited sources. Trust is the
umbrella; the others feed it.

What it looks like on the page, in fixable terms:

- **Experience**: proof of first-hand contact with the subject — original
  photos, real numbers from the owner's own work, specifics no outsider
  would know. This is where small sites can beat big publishers.
- **Expertise**: a named author with a reason to be believed, stated where
  the reader can see it; claims sourced; content kept current, with dates
  that are honest (redating unchanged content is a trust burn).
- **Authoritativeness**: being referenced by others in the field — earned
  off-page (see the research reference of this skill), reflected on-page by "as featured
  in" only when true.
- **Trust**: a real organization visibly behind the site — contact page
  with an address, who-we-are, policies where money changes hands, working
  customer service paths, no dark patterns. Errors readings that show broken
  checkout or form flows are trust damage in the most literal sense.

Honest limits: E-E-A-T improvements are slow, sitewide, and unprovable in
isolation — never promise that adding author bios will move a specific rank.
Frame it as removing reasons to be distrusted, on the pages where money or
wellbeing is at stake first.

## Rewrite, add, or consolidate — the decision

- Page ranks on the cusp (page-two territory in rank data) with the right
  intent: improve that page — sharpen the title against the winning pattern,
  add what the top results cover that it lacks, add internal links from
  strong pages. Never delete-and-recreate; edit in place and keep the URL.
- Page ranks nowhere and the intent is wrong: build the right-shaped page
  and let each page own its intent.
- Multiple pages fight for one query: consolidate to the strongest URL.
- After any content change, judge in rank data over weeks, not days, and
  read heatmaps on the changed page to see whether people now reach the
  substance. One change per page per measurement window, or attribution is
  guesswork.
