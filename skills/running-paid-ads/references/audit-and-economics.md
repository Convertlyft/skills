# Reading the Money, Pacing the Budget, Matching the Message, Auditing Waste

Depth for four jobs: reading ROAS / CPL / CPA properly, judging budget and
pacing, working message match, and running a full wasteful-campaign audit.
Every judgment here is relative to the account's own economics and history -
never to invented benchmarks.

## Reading ROAS, CPL, and CPA properly

### Start from value, not cost

- **For e-commerce**: the break-even ROAS is set by gross margin. Revenue
  divided by spend must clear the point where margin on that revenue covers
  the spend. Ask for margin before praising or condemning any ROAS. Repeat
  purchase behavior raises what a first sale is worth; if the owner knows
  their repeat rate, first-purchase ROAS can be judged against lifetime
  value rather than a single order.
- **For lead generation**: cost per lead must be chained through to money:
  cost per lead, times leads per closed deal, against value per closed
  deal. A campaign can halve its CPL by attracting worse leads and be worse
  off. Whenever the owner can label lead outcomes, judge cost per closed
  deal, not cost per form fill.
- **ROAS is a ratio and hides scale.** A spectacular return on tiny spend
  and a modest return on large spend are different businesses. Returns fall
  as spend scales into weaker demand - expect it, and look for the level
  where marginal return crosses break-even, not the average.

### Attribution honesty

- Platform ROAS is self-graded homework: each platform claims conversions
  by its own window and rules, view-through included on some. Summing
  platform-reported conversions across channels overstates reality.
- The sturdiest simple check is blended: total ad spend against total new
  customers or revenue in the period, from the owner's own books. When
  platform numbers and blended reality diverge widely, trust the books and
  say the platform view is optimistic.
- Brand campaigns claim people who already chose the business. Their
  reported performance is always stellar and mostly not incremental. Judge
  the account with brand split out, and treat "should we pay for our own
  brand" as a separate question: worth paying when competitors bid on the
  name or the ad owns meaningfully more of the page than the organic
  listing; worth testing with a pause geo-by-geo or period-by-period when
  in doubt - rank data shows whether the organic listing stands ready
  underneath.

### Statistical humility

- Never conclude from a handful of conversions. Before declaring a winner
  or a failure, ask whether a few conversions moving either way would flip
  the verdict; if yes, the data has not spoken yet.
- Compare like periods (weekday mix, seasonality, promotions). The most
  common false alarm is comparing a short recent window against a longer
  or different-shaped one.
- Conversion lag makes the most recent days incomplete in every report.
  Exclude them or say the caveat out loud.

## Budget and pacing

- Daily budgets are paced to the month: single-day overshoots are normal
  and self-correcting. Judge spend at the week or month level.
- **Diagnose "limited by budget" before adding money.** If search terms
  contain waste, a budget raise buys more waste. Clean first, then fund.
- **Lost impression share splits into two diseases.** Share lost to budget:
  demand exists that money is not funding - concentrate spend on the
  best-performing campaign rather than sprinkling raises everywhere. Share
  lost to rank: ads lose auctions on quality and bid - more budget does
  not help; better ads, tighter relevance, or higher targets do.
- **Concentration beats fragmentation.** Automated bidding learns per
  campaign or ad set from its own conversions. Many small budgets starve
  every learner. Merging overlapping campaigns pools signal and usually
  steadies performance.
- Raise or cut budgets in modest steps, not doublings, and let automated
  bidding resettle between moves; large jolts restart learning.
- Dayparting and geo bid adjustments should follow evidence from the
  account's own segmented results over a fair sample - not folklore about
  when customers browse. Automated bidding already adjusts for time and
  place internally; manual layers on top mostly fight it.

## Message match - depth

The chain to keep intact: the search or the scroll-stopping hook, the ad's
promise, the landing page's first screen, the conversion ask. Any broken
link leaks paid money.

- **The first screen must repay the click.** The specific thing promised -
  offer, product, price frame, location - visible without scrolling, in
  words close to the ad's own. People do not read pages looking for the
  promise; they glance, fail to find it, and leave.
- **Specificity beats the homepage.** Every distinct intent deserves the
  most specific page that serves it. The homepage is the right destination
  only for brand searches.
- **One page per ad group** is the practical discipline: if two ad groups
  share a landing page honestly, they may be one ad group; if one ad group
  needs two pages, it is two ad groups.
- **Continuity of language matters as much as content**: matching phrasing
  between ad and headline reassures the person they are in the right
  place. Dynamic keyword insertion fakes this cheaply and reads as spam
  when queries are messy - prefer honest per-ad-group copy.
- **Evidence over opinion**: heatmaps on a paid landing page
  show whether people engage with the promise block or hunt elsewhere;
  replays show hesitation and pogo behavior; funnels show whether the page
  moves paid visitors to the next step or sheds them at arrival. A high
  drop at the landing step with clean search terms is a message-match or
  page problem, not a keyword problem.

## Auditing a wasteful campaign - the walkthrough

Run in order; each step can end the audit early with a finding.

1. **Verify the conversion.** What event is counted, is it primary, is it
   deduplicated, does it reconcile against an independent source over a
   full recent month? If tracking fails here, stop: the finding is "fix
   tracking, then re-audit" - no performance conclusion is safe.
2. **Establish break-even.** Margin or lead-to-deal chain, from the owner.
   Write it down; every later judgment refers to it.
3. **Split brand from non-brand** and restate performance both ways. Much
   apparent health evaporates here, and that is a finding in itself.
4. **Rank spend by destination.** Campaigns by cost, then within the
   heaviest campaigns, ad groups and search terms (or Meta breakdowns:
   placement, audience, creative) by cost. Audit the top spenders first -
   waste concentrates.
5. **Read the search terms** for the heavy spenders against buyable
   intent. Harvest: negatives to add, match types to tighten, ad groups to
   split where one group serves mixed intents.
6. **Check the silent settings**: search partners and Display expansion,
   auto-applied recommendations, the change history for changes nobody
   remembers, counting mode, attribution window.
7. **Follow the click** for the heaviest ad groups: does the landing page
   keep the promise on the first screen; do funnels, heatmaps, replays,
   and error readings show the page doing its job? A broken or mismatched
   page reframes the whole audit.
8. **Judge bidding fit**: strategy versus conversion volume (starved smart
   bidding thrashes), targets versus recent actuals (an aggressive target
   quietly strangles delivery), learning-period churn from frequent edits.
9. **Write findings as money.** Rank by estimated spend affected - "the
   largest share of non-brand spend goes to terms that cannot convert"
   outranks "ad copy could be fresher". Attach the evidence for each
   finding and the single next action.

## When not to spend - expanded

Advising zero is a legitimate expert output. The cases:

- **Blind spending.** Tracking broken or unverifiable: pause, fix, then
  return. Money spent blind cannot even buy learning.
- **A page that cannot convert.** Evidence first: near-total funnel
  drop-off, error readings on the conversion path, replays showing people
  failing at the form. Ads amplify the page; amplifying failure costs
  double - the spend and the reputation with the platform's quality
  systems.
- **Arithmetic that cannot close.** When margin times any plausible
  conversion rate cannot cover real click prices in this market, the
  problem is the offer or the economics, not the campaign settings. Say
  so plainly.
- **No demand to harvest, nothing to create.** No search volume for the
  offer and no concrete, feed-stoppable proposition for social means
  paid channels have nothing to work with yet.
- **A budget below the price of learning.** If plausible click prices mean
  the budget buys only a dribble of clicks a day, no statistically
  readable result will ever arrive; the same money saved up and spent as
  one concentrated test on one campaign, one offer, one page, teaches
  something real.
- **During a broken window.** Site migrations, checkout rebuilds, tracking
  rebuilds: pause paid traffic through the turbulence rather than paying
  to send people into it.

The alternative to spending is never "nothing": fix the page (evidence
already in hand from behaviour readings), build the organic position
rank data shows is close, or bank the budget until a real test is
affordable.
